Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises over time. If a basket of everyday goods cost ₹1,000 last year and costs ₹1,050 today, inflation is 5%.

The key word is *general*. One price going up, such as tomatoes in a bad season, is not inflation. Inflation means prices are rising across many goods and services at once.

## How inflation is measured

Governments track a fixed "basket" of items that households typically buy: food, fuel, housing, clothes, transport, education and more. Each item gets a weight based on how much people spend on it. The index built from this basket is called the Consumer Price Index (CPI).

![Bars rising over time](../../assets/uploads/inflation.jpg "Rising prices: the same basket costs more each year.")

## Why prices rise

Economists usually point to two broad causes:

- **Demand-pull:** people want to buy more than the economy can produce, so sellers raise prices.
- **Cost-push:** the cost of making things goes up (fuel, wages, imported materials), and businesses pass it on.

> Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon.

Milton Friedman's famous line captures one school of thought: too much money chasing too few goods. Many economists think the full story is broader, but money supply certainly matters.

## Who gains and who loses

### Losers

People whose income does not rise as fast as prices, and savers who keep cash in an account that pays less interest than the inflation rate. Their money buys less each year.

### Winners

Borrowers with fixed-rate loans, because they repay in money that is worth less than when they borrowed it.

## What central banks do

Central banks try to keep inflation low and stable. India's Reserve Bank of India works with an inflation target of 4%, with a tolerance band of 2% to 6%. Its main tool is the interest rate: higher rates make borrowing costlier, cool spending, and slow price rises.

## What you can do

1. Compare your savings interest rate with the current inflation rate.
2. Look for investments that have historically kept pace with prices over the long run.
3. Ask for raises that at least match inflation.
