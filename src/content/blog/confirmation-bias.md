Confirmation bias is the tendency to look for, notice and remember information that supports what we already think, while ignoring or explaining away what does not.

It is not a sign of stupidity. It is a mental shortcut that helps us make quick decisions. The problem is that it also makes us overconfident.

## A classic experiment

In 1960, psychologist Peter Wason gave people three numbers: 2, 4, 6. He told them the numbers followed a rule and asked them to discover it by proposing new sets of three, which he would mark as fitting or not.

Most people guessed "numbers going up by two" and then tested only examples that fit, such as 8, 10, 12. Each time the answer was "yes", so they felt certain. The real rule was just "any increasing numbers". Hardly anyone tried a set that could *break* their guess, such as 1, 2, 3 or 10, 7, 4.

## Where it shows up

- **News and social media:** we follow sources we already agree with.
- **Health:** we remember the times a remedy "worked" and forget the times it did not.
- **Work:** after forming a first impression of a colleague, we notice behaviour that confirms it.
- **Money:** investors read only good news about stocks they own.

> The question is not "Can I find evidence that I am right?" but "What would show me I am wrong?"

## How to reduce it

1. **Argue the other side.** Write down the best case against your view.
2. **Look for disconfirming evidence first.** Search for the reasons you might be mistaken.
3. **Slow down.** Big decisions deserve a night's sleep.
4. **Ask someone who disagrees,** and listen without defending.
5. **Write predictions in advance.** You can then check them honestly later.

## The takeaway

You cannot switch confirmation bias off, but you can build small habits that catch it. Being willing to change your mind is a skill, and it improves with practice.
